## Introduction: What Are Brand Voice & Tone Guidelines?

Think of these guidelines as your roadmap to sounding like us (not like every other care provider, and definitely not like a corporate manual).

At Continuity of Care Services, we built everything around one simple truth: **Your Team, Your Time, Your Life**. These guidelines make sure that promise comes through in every email, every webpage, every conversation.

### What they are

Your go-to reference for writing like Continuity of Care Services. Whether you're drafting a quick email or rewriting the homepage, you'll know what to say and how to say it.

### Their purpose

Stop families scrolling past generic care provider websites. Make them think, "These people get it." Build trust before we even meet.

### How they help

- No more staring at blank pages wondering "Does this sound right?"
- New team members sound like they've been here for years
- Families recognize us across every channel (website, email, phone call)
- We stand out in a sea of identical "compassionate care" claims

### Why we're using them

Because care decisions are terrifying. Families are overwhelmed, guilty, scared. The last thing they need is more corporate-speak or medical jargon. They need to hear from real people who understand what they're going through.

That's us. These guidelines make sure it always sounds like us.

## Voice & Tone Guiding Principles

Our voice is who we are. Our tone is how we adapt that personality to different situations. These four principles guide all Continuity of Care Services communication:

### 1. Compassionate Warmth

**What it means:**

We sound like a friend who's been through this before. Not a customer service script. Not a brochure. A real person who gets how hard this is.

**How it affects our writing:**

- Cut the corporate fluff. If you wouldn't say it to your own mum, don't write it
- Use their name. Say "you" and "your loved one," not "clients" or "service users"
- Write about morning tea and comfortable slippers, not "personal care interventions"
- Admit when things are tough. Don't sugarcoat with empty positivity
- Remember: they're trusting us with someone they love. Write like you understand that weight

**Example copy:**

> "Your carer won't change every week. Same person, same time, same chat about the garden. They'll know how you like your tea (two sugars, none of that sweetener nonsense) and what helps when your hip's playing up."

**What not to do:**

- Sound like a robot reading from a policy manual
- Rush them through big decisions
- Use words like "service delivery" or "care provision"
- Pretend everything's always sunshine and rainbows

**Incorrect copy examples:**

- ❌ "Service provision scheduled daily. All procedures routine and documented."
- ❌ "Our operatives deliver standardised care packages to end users."
- ❌ "Client interface facilitated through optimized scheduling protocols."

---

### 2. Personal Dignity & Respect

**What it means:**

Nobody's helpless. Nobody's a burden. Everyone deserves to live their way, in their home, on their terms.

**How it affects our writing:**

- Focus on what people want to keep doing, not what they "can't do anymore"
- Say "support" not "take over"
- Write about choice and independence, not dependence and limitations
- Never talk about people like they're not in the room
- Respect that this is their life. We're guests, helping them live it

**Example copy:**

> "Stay in your own home. Keep your routines. Do what you love. We're just here to help with the bits that have got harder. Everything else stays yours."

**What not to do:**

- Talk about people in the third person when addressing families ("they" instead of "your dad")
- Assume anything about what someone wants or needs
- Use words that strip away autonomy ("managed," "supervised," "monitored")
- Write like age or disability makes someone less of a person

**Incorrect copy examples:**

- ❌ "We'll take over all daily activities for your elderly relative."
- ❌ "Patients are assessed and given appropriate levels of supervision."
- ❌ "Full-time monitoring ensures compliance with care protocols."

---

### 3. Genuine Optimism

**What it means:**

Yeah, getting older is hard. Care needs are stressful. We're not here to pretend otherwise. But there's still joy, still laughter, still good days worth having.

**How it affects our writing:**

- Be honest about challenges, then show what's still possible
- Notice the small stuff: a good cup of tea, the garden coming back, a favourite show
- Write about living, not just existing
- Acknowledge fear and worry, but don't camp out there
- Balance realism with hope (without toxic positivity)

**Example copy:**

> "Some days are tougher than others. Your carer gets that. They're there for the tricky mornings and the rough afternoons, but also for the moment you both crack up laughing about something silly on the telly."

**What not to do:**

- Pretend everything's always brilliant ("Every day is a blessing!")
- Focus only on decline and medical needs
- Make unrealistic promises about outcomes
- Use fake cheerfulness that ignores real difficulties

**Incorrect copy examples:**

- ❌ "As health inevitably declines, we manage deterioration through evidence-based interventions."
- ❌ "Our services continue until end-of-life outcomes are achieved."
- ❌ "Progressive condition management for advancing stages of dependency."

---

### 4. Skilled Support

**What it means:**

We know what we're doing. You can trust us. But we're not going to bore you with credentials or hide behind jargon.

**How it affects our writing:**

- Mention training and standards, but keep it brief and human
- Show expertise through specific details, not vague claims
- Use words like "skilled" and "expert" sparingly (mostly, just demonstrate it)
- Balance professionalism with approachability (we're competent and kind)
- Never sound pompous or talk down from expertise

**Example copy:**

> "Complex care at home: epilepsy, PEG feeding, mobility support. Our team's trained, your family's involved, everything's coordinated. No drama, just good care."

**What not to do:**

- List every qualification and certification like it's a CV
- Use medical jargon to sound impressive
- Be so professional you sound cold
- Make families feel stupid for not understanding technical terms

**Incorrect copy examples:**

- ❌ "Our Level 5 Diploma-certified operatives implement evidence-based care modalities per CQC framework standards."
- ❌ "Regulatory compliance monitoring conducted through quarterly audit cycles. Full documentation archived per statutory requirements."
- ❌ "Staff credentialed across multiple care protocols with continuing professional development requirements."

## Vocabulary Guidelines

### Words That Sound Like Us

**People:**
- You, your dad, your mum, your loved one, families
- Carers (not "caregivers" or "support workers")
- Team, people, everyone

**What We Do:**
- Help, support, step in, show up
- Know, understand, get, remember
- Keep, stay, continue, maintain

**How It Feels:**
- Home (not "property" or "residence")
- Comfortable, familiar, easy
- Trust, peace, relief
- Light (as in "make things lighter," not "illuminate")

**Time:**
- Your time, your pace, your rhythm
- Regular (not "scheduled")
- When you need it, day or night

**Quality:**
- Proper, decent, solid, good
- Real, genuine, actual
- Right (as in "feels right")

### Words That Don't Sound Like Us

**Corporate Speak:**
- Deliver, provide, facilitate, enable
- Solutions, offerings, provisions
- Stakeholders, end-users, service users
- Journey, touchpoint, ecosystem

**Medical Jargon:**
- Domiciliary, residential, institutional
- ADLs (just say what they are)
- Intervention, modality, protocol
- Patient, resident, beneficiary

**Business Buzzwords:**
- Holistic, bespoke, tailored (overused)
- Innovative, cutting-edge, revolutionary
- Empowering, transformative, impactful
- Curated, optimized, leveraged

**Euphemisms:**
- Passing away, end-of-life journey
- Differently abled, mobility challenged
- Golden years, senior lifestyle
- (Use plain language: dying, disabled, getting older)

## Tone Adjustments by Context

Our voice stays the same (straightforward, warm, no nonsense) but we shift tone depending on where people meet us.

### Website

Welcoming but not salesy. "Care That Feels Like Family in Maidstone & Kent." Focus on what life looks like with us, not a list of services.

### Email

Keep it personal and useful. Get to the point, give clear next steps, don't bury the important bit in paragraph three. "Get in touch today, and we'll work with you and your family to create a care plan that's unique to you."

### Customer Service

Lead with listening. Someone's ringing because something's not right; acknowledge that first, fix it second. Patient, never rushed. "If it all feels a bit overwhelming, don't worry. Our friendly team is just a phone call away."

### Educational Content (blogs, guides)

Clear without being patronising. Break down the complicated stuff, but don't talk down. "Discover the care and support we bring to homes across Maidstone & Kent."

### Social Media

Bit more relaxed, shorter sentences, occasional emoji if it fits. Still professional (we're not trying to be your mate, just less formal). Community-focused.

### Crisis Situations

Drop the casual. More serious, more formal, but still human. Clear, direct, focused on solutions. "We're here to help during this difficult time. Let's talk about how we can support you."

### Internal Comms

Bit more professional than external, but same basic voice. Recognise what people do well, be clear about expectations, reinforce why we do things the way we do.

## Grammar & Style Conventions

### Contractions

- Use contractions to sound conversational and approachable: "we're," "you're," "it's," "don't," "can't"
- Exception: Avoid in very formal contexts like legal documents or crisis communications

### Punctuation

- Use the Oxford comma for clarity: "care, support, and comfort"
- Use **em dashes—like this—**to add warmth and conversational flow
- Use ellipses sparingly... and only when natural
- Exclamation points: Use sparingly and only when genuinely enthusiastic (mostly in social media)

### Capitalization

- Capitalize "Continuity of Care Services" when referring to the company
- Lowercase "carer" and "care professional" unless starting a sentence
- Capitalize service names: "Complex Care," "Respite Care" (when referring to specific service offerings)
- Use sentence case for headers and subheaders

### Numbers

- Spell out numbers one through nine
- Use numerals for 10 and above
- Use numerals for time: "24/7," "9am"
- Spell out "per cent" in body copy, use "%" in headlines and data

### Sentence Structure

- Average length: 15-20 words for body copy
- Mix short and long sentences for natural rhythm
- Use active voice by default: "We provide care" not "Care is provided"
- Start sentences with "And" or "But" when it feels natural
- Use sentence fragments occasionally for emphasis. Like this.

### Paragraph Length

- Keep paragraphs short: 2-4 sentences for web content
- Single-sentence paragraphs are acceptable for emphasis
- Longer paragraphs (5-6 sentences) acceptable for print materials

### Lists & Formatting

- Use bullets for unordered information
- Use numbers for sequential steps or rankings
- Keep list items parallel in structure
- Introduce lists with complete sentences ending in a colon

## Cadence & Rhythm

Read everything aloud. If it sounds like a PowerPoint presentation, bin it. If it sounds like someone explaining something clearly without showing off, you're probably there.

### Good Rhythm Examples

**Example 1:**
> "Same carer every week. Not random faces who don't know if you take sugar. Someone who remembers what you were watching last time, asks after your grandson, notices when your hip's worse."

That works because: short opener (Same carer every week), specific detail (the sugar thing), then builds with examples that feel real.

**Example 2:**
> "We don't do that thing where carers rotate constantly. You know, stranger every other day, explaining the same stuff over and over. Exhausting. Instead, one person. Your person. They learn your routine, your quirks, what makes a good day better and a bad day manageable."

That works because it acknowledges the annoying thing first (rotating carers), labels it (exhausting), then offers the alternative.

**Opening a page without faff:**
> "Care at home. Proper care, from people who show up and care. No agency nonsense (rotating carers who don't know you). Just good, consistent support that fits around your life, not the other way round."

Gets straight to it. No "Welcome to our comprehensive care services" rubbish.

**Explaining technical stuff:**
> "Complex care sounds scary. PEG feeding, epilepsy management, mobility support... it's a lot. But our team does this stuff every day. They'll work with you, your family, your nurses. Keep things running smoothly so you can focus on living, not coordinating."

Acknowledges it's intimidating, doesn't pretend otherwise, then makes it sound manageable.

**Ending without pressure:**
> "Questions? Course you do. Give us a ring. No pressure, just straight answers."

Short, assumes they'll have questions (everyone does), makes it easy to take the next step.

## Channel-Specific Applications

### Website Copy

- Clear value propositions on the homepage, warm welcome
- About page tells our story with authenticity
- Service pages balance technical details with emotional benefits
- FAQ answers questions helpfully and reassuringly
- Contact page makes reaching out feel easy and comfortable

### UX/Microcopy

- Button text should be action-oriented but friendly: "Get in Touch," "Discover Our Care," "Learn More"
- Form labels stay clear and conversational: "Your Name," "How Can We Help?"
- Error messages are helpful, never blaming: "Oops! Please enter your email address."
- Success messages are warm and affirming: "Thanks! We'll be in touch soon."

### Email Marketing

- Subject lines stay personal and benefit-focused, 35-50 characters
- Preview text extends the subject line naturally
- Body copy is conversational, scannable, focused on one clear message
- CTAs are clear and action-oriented, surrounded by white space

### Social Media

- Tone's more casual but never unprofessional
- Keep it concise (but not at the expense of warmth)
- Respond promptly, personally, and warmly
- Hashtags sparingly and naturally: #HomeCareKent #FamilyFirst

### Print Materials

- Brochures can be more comprehensive, slightly more formal but still warm
- Direct mail stays personal, benefits-focused, with clear call-to-action
- Advertising is concise, memorable, emotionally resonant

## Do's and Don'ts

### Do

- Sound like a real person
- Use "you" and mean them specifically
- Write short sentences most of the time
- Cut corporate words (if it sounds like LinkedIn, delete it)
- Be honest about the hard stuff
- Notice small details: tea preferences, TV shows, garden chat
- Use contractions
- End paragraphs before people's eyes glaze over
- Read it aloud. Does it sound weird? Then it is
- Keep coming back to: **Your Team, Your Time, Your Life**

### Don't

- Say "clients" (use their name, your dad, your mum, you)
- Write like you're filling in a form
- Use words like "facilitate" or "deliver" or "stakeholder"
- Talk about "care journeys" or "service touchpoints"
- Pretend everything's always wonderful
- List qualifications like it's a CV
- Use more words than needed (half of them are probably waffle)
- Sound chipper when someone's having a genuinely awful time
- Make promises you can't keep
- Forget that real people (scared, tired, overwhelmed people) are reading this

## Example Transformations

### Cold and Clinical

**Before:**
> "Continuity of Care Services provides domiciliary care provision to clients requiring assistance with ADLs. Service delivery scheduled per care plan parameters."

**After (Our voice):**
> "We bring care and support to your home, helping with everyday activities like getting dressed, making meals, and staying comfortable. All scheduled around your routine, not ours."

### Impersonal

**Before:**
> "Staff will be assigned to client based on availability. Standard protocols will be followed."

**After (Our voice):**
> "You'll have a dedicated carer who gets to know you (not just your care needs, but what makes you smile and brings you comfort)."

### Negative Framing

**Before:**
> "Due to cognitive decline and mobility impairment, patient requires full assistance."

**After (Our voice):**
> "We provide expert support that helps you maintain your independence and dignity at home, with gentle assistance tailored to your needs."

### Corporate Waffle

**Before:**
> "We leverage our extensive expertise to facilitate optimal outcomes through person-centred care delivery solutions."

**After (Our voice):**
> "Good care from people who know what they're doing."

## Voice Evolution & Maintenance

### When to Review These Guidelines

- Annually as part of brand strategy review
- When entering new markets or service areas
- After significant company growth or restructuring
- When customer feedback indicates messaging inconsistency
- When launching major new marketing campaigns

### Keeping Guidelines Current

- Collect examples of great voice usage
- Document new approved phrases and terminology
- Update based on customer language research
- Track competitive positioning shifts
- Monitor cultural and social changes affecting language

### Training New Team Members

- All new hires should read these guidelines in first week
- Conduct voice training workshop for customer-facing roles
- Provide before/after examples for practice
- Assign voice "mentors" for new writers
- Review voice consistency in first 90 days

## Conclusion

These guidelines aren't about ticking boxes. They're about making sure that when someone's looking for care (probably terrified, definitely exhausted) they read our stuff and think "Finally, someone who talks like a human being."

**Your Team, Your Time, Your Life** isn't a slogan. It's what we mean. These guidelines make sure we sound like we mean it.

Every email. Every webpage. Every phone call. People should come away feeling heard (not processed), respected (not patronised), hopeful without being bullshitted, and safe without being sold to.

### When you're not sure if something sounds right:

1. Would I say this to my own family?
2. Is this how I actually talk, or am I doing that corporate voice thing?
3. If I was scared and overwhelmed, would this help or just add more noise?

Answer those honestly and you'll probably write something good.

**Questions?** Email [marketing contact] or grab someone who's been here a while. These guidelines help, but sometimes you'll need to break the "rules" to sound like a real person. That's usually the right call.
